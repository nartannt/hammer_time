\section{Introduction}
Lists of some of the more popular generalist proof assistants are often accompanied by a short summary of their most salient strength or feature. Lean is rarely talked of without mention of Mathlib, Rocq often discussed in relation to its theoretical foundations. When it comes to Isabelle/HOL, its powerful automation, in the form of Sledgehammer, is often touted as one of its core strengths. The LeanHammer project is a tool which implements a similar approach in the Lean proof assistant. In order to acheive this, it relies on the following components:
\begin{enumerate}
   \item Its own premise selector to identify the lemmas and definitions which are likely to be necessary to prove a given theorem or lemma.
   \item lean-auto: a translation procedure fron Lean's logic into monomorphic higher-ordrer logic.
   \item External theorem provers (namely Zipperposition and SMT solvers).
   \item Duper: an automatic theorem prover written in Lean that serves to reconstruct a proof of the goal from the output of the external theorem provers.
\end{enumerate}

Although the architecture of LeanHammer closely resembles that of Sledgehammer, it is necessary to note that Lean is a far more hostile environment to this kind of automation. 
Firstly, Lean's logic is an extension of dependent type theory as opposed to Isabelle's extension of higher-order logic. An immediate consequence of this is that the translation work of lean-auto needs to be significantly more involved to reach the logic supported by Zipperposition.
Secondly, Lean's handling of equality implies that deciding the definitional equality a pair of terms can be computationally intensive. As a consequence, it is often impractical to establish all true equalities between the terms of a given problem [TODO am i speaking out of line here?].
Thirdly, Lean's typeclass system relies on an often expensive inference system. This affects premise selection, which cannot inspect all possibly relevant lemmas without paying the full cost of the full typeclass resolution algorithm.
Finally, the sheer size of Mathlib, has dire implications for premise selection because it acts as a massive multiplier on costs, whether they come in the form of caching or time.

These challenges likely explain why LeanHammer hasn't had the same success in the Lean community as Sledgehammer among Isabelle users. However, we also believe there is room for improvements. In this paper, we present a novel extension of a light-weight premise selection algorithm and changes to lean-auto's TPTP encoding and Zipperposition invocation. We evaluate these changes and show that their cumulative effect allows for a modest improvement in the hammer's overall performance and responsiveness.

MePo is a very lightweight premise selection procedure, it is a score based system. A hypothesis will be given a score of \(\frac{g}{c}\) where \(c\) is the number of constants in the hypothesis and \(g\) the number of constants which appear in both the hypothesis and the goal. The current version of MePo also implements a more sophisticated version of this procedure which weights the score of a given constant by how rarely this constant occurs in the goal. This approach performs remarkably well considering its simplicity (and more to the point: its economy). However, it does not perform well-enough to compete with the LeanHammer selection procedure. We therefore implemented several additional heuristic refinements, in the same vein as the rarity weighting. One major constraint on any change to MePo is efficiency, our goal is to save time with regards to the existing default. 

We made four main changes to MePo, presented in the following sub-sections.

\subsection{Polarity}

Consider the problem \(P \Rightarrow Q \), the hypotheses \(P, \lnot P, Q\) and \(\lnot Q\) would be given the same weight by standard MePo. It is clear that only hypotheses \(P\) and \(\lnot Q\) are useful to show this goal. Indeed, a symbol that appears positively in a hypothesis will only be useful in a proof if appears negatively in the goal because it otherwise gives no additional information. This feature consists in a simple change, each constant of the goal carries the information of the polarity at which it occurs in the goal, a matching constant in a candidate hypothesis will only be counted in the score if it occurs with the opposite polariy.

Computing the polarity of a constant in a higher-order formula is undecidable because propositional symbols can be quantified over and constrained with arbitrary higher-order formulas. Furthermore, some propositional symbols erase polarity, this is trivially the case for \(\lambda x : \text{Prop}. \;\bot\). This can easily be overcome by adding the possibility of constants to be of undetermined polarity and allowing a match to be counted if either constant involved is of unknown polarity.

Once this subtelty is accounted for, we are left with a change that can eliminate obviously spurious constant matches which occur above non-polar propositional symbols. Most theorems and lemmas are more likely to make use of the standard porpositional connectives over newly defined ones, giving grounds to believe this heuristic should be reasonably effective. In fact, given the very low computational overhead of this feature, it would take very little additonal performance to justify its inclusion.
